 Abstract
 Financial transaction monitoring involves a trade-off between detecting rare adverse events and managing
 investigation costs. This study develops Project Sentinel, a reproducible synthetic payment analytics
 framework, to compare Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost and Isolation Forest using
 chronological validation, probability calibration, cost-sensitive threshold selection and independent Monte
 Carlo replication. Four sequential experiments use 31 distinct simulation seeds, each generating 100,000
 fictional payment attempts (3.1 million generated records across distinct datasets; the initial dataset is reused
 in stages I and II). Stage III evaluates four rolling test windows per seed across ten fresh seeds; stage IV
 compares Logistic Regression and XGBoost across twenty additional seeds using matched original and
 alternative label mechanisms. Under baseline hypothetical costs, stage III finds that XGBoost costs KES
 440.62 less per 1,000 attempts than Random Forest (95% CI: 295.34–585.90 less). Stage IV finds XGBoost
 costs KES 216.00 more than Logistic Regression (95% CI: 26.16–405.84 more), whereas the difference under
 the alternative mechanism is inconclusive (−4.00; 95% CI: −177.54–169.54). Calibration improves Brier
 scores substantially; recent transaction count is the largest mean absolute raw-log-odds attribution in both final
 models. The results support conditional, not universal, advantages of simpler statistical models. Low recall
 and simulated labels preclude production fraud claims. The framework contributes a transparent experimental
 approach to evaluating payment risk models under operational constraints.
 Keywords: payment operations; synthetic data; cost-sensitive learning; temporal validation; probability
 calibration; explainable AI; financial risk.

 1. Introduction
 Digital payment systems generate transaction attempts, processing outcomes and settlement records that can
 support operational monitoring. Risk classification poses a distinct challenge: positive events may be rare,
 alerts consume investigative capacity, and missed positives have consequences. A model with a strong ranking
 metric may nevertheless be expensive to operate at a poorly chosen threshold. Sentinel separates operational
 measurement from experimental risk prediction and evaluates the latter through explicit financial assumptions.
 The research asks whether model complexity produces a repeatable economic advantage after calibration and
 chronological validation, and whether that advantage persists under a changed synthetic risk mechanism. The
 study is a simulation experiment, not an empirical estimate of fraud in Kenya or any named payment provider.






 2. Literature and analytical foundation
 Random Forest aggregates randomized trees to capture nonlinear patterns (Breiman, 2001). XGBoost uses
 scalable gradient-boosted trees (Chen & Guestrin, 2016). Logistic Regression provides a lower-complexity
 conditional probability benchmark; Isolation Forest provides an anomaly-scoring alternative. These model
 families serve different purposes, so their comparison must account for class imbalance and the intended
 decision policy rather than accuracy alone.
 Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015) show why precision–recall analysis is particularly informative for imbalanced
 classification. Average precision (AP) summarizes ranking performance over recall levels and is interpreted
 relative to positive prevalence. ROC-AUC remains a complementary measure of discrimination but does not
 directly express alert quality at a selected threshold. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) demonstrate the
 importance of evaluating and calibrating probability estimates; Sentinel consequently distinguishes probability
 quality from ranking and threshold-specific performance.
 Lundberg and Lee (2017) introduce SHAP as a framework for assigning feature contributions to predictions.
 Here explanations are used to examine whether the final models consistently attribute importance to simulated
 behavioural signals, rather than establish causal relationships. The present study integrates these established
 methods with an explicit review-cost objective and repeated chronological simulation. It does not claim to
 introduce a new machine learning algorithm or establish a gap through an exhaustive systematic review.

 3. Research objectives and hypotheses
 The study has four objectives: (1) construct a reproducible synthetic payment operations dataset and quantify
 provider and settlement metrics; (2) compare statistical and machine learning models using temporally
 separated fitting, calibration, threshold selection and testing; (3) quantify the reliability of economic
 comparisons across fresh simulation seeds and alternative assumptions; and (4) assess calibration, feature
 attribution stability and robustness under an alternative risk mechanism.
 The pre-specified stage III contrast is the mean test cost of XGBoost minus Random Forest, normalized per
 1,000 attempts. The stage IV primary contrast is XGBoost minus Logistic Regression under the original
 synthetic mechanism. The null hypothesis for each paired contrast is a zero mean difference; the alternative is
 nonzero. A negative difference favours XGBoost. A two-sided approximate 95% Student t interval is
 calculated across independent seed-level differences. A stage IV descriptive practical-similarity margin of
 ±KES 250 per 1,000 is analyst-chosen, not a formally approved equivalence bound.

 4. Methodology
 4.1 Design and data generation
 The study uses sequential quantitative Monte Carlo experiments. Each dataset contains 100,000 fictional
 payment attempts with simulated timestamps in 2025, four fictional PSPs, three payment methods, deposits
 and withdrawals, KES amounts, account age, attempts, recent transaction counts, processing time, status and
 settlement values. Transaction amounts are sampled from a clipped lognormal distribution; retry and velocity
 variables are Poisson-based. Provider-specific failure probabilities and simplified settlement deviations are
 generated separately from the risk labels. All data are synthetic.






 4.2 Synthetic label mechanism and features
 The original binary risk label is sampled from a Bernoulli distribution with probability given by the logistic
 transformation of the following simulated score:
 z = −5.4 + 0.30v + 0.85I(a < 30) + 0.80I(r > 3) + 0.70I(m > 15,000) + ε, where ε ~ N(0, 0.8²), v denotes recent
 transaction count, a account age in days, r attempt count, and m transaction amount. I(.) is an indicator
 function. The alternative stress mechanism uses z = −4.9 + 0.15v + 0.40I(a < 30) + 0.40I(r > 3) + 0.35I(m >
 15,000) + 0.80I(withdrawal) + ε, with ε ~ N(0, 1²). The stress mechanism is deliberately illustrative and does
 not represent observed fraud drift.
 Stage I uses amount, attempt count, account age and recent transaction count. Subsequent stages add log(1 +
 amount), a new-account indicator and the product of attempt count and recent transaction count. Settlement,
 transaction outcome and risk label are excluded from model inputs. This avoids direct outcome leakage in the
 simulated design; actual availability of retry and recent-transaction features at a decision timestamp would
 need verification before real deployment.

 Stage III uses seed values 101, 211, 307, 401, 503, 601, 701, 809, 907 and 1009. Training expands from four

 both replication experiments.
 4.4 Models and calibration
 Logistic Regression uses standardized features, balanced class weights and a maximum of 1,000 iterations.
 Random Forest uses 100 trees, depth 10, minimum leaf size 15 and balanced class weights. XGBoost uses 150
 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.05, row and column subsampling 0.85 and a training negative-to-positive weight
 ratio. Isolation Forest uses 100 trees and is fitted without labels; its anomaly score is subsequently calibrated
 using labelled calibration observations. Each model uses random state 42. In stages II–IV a separate logistic
 mapping calibrates the model score using a disjoint chronological calibration set.






 4.5 Cost-sensitive decision policy and inference
 Let TP, FP and FN denote true positives, false positives and false negatives. The hypothetical decision loss is
 L = c■(TP + FP) + c■FN. The baseline assumes review cost c■ = KES 25 and missed-positive cost c■ =
 KES 1,000. The no-alert policy incurs c■ times the number of positives. The validation threshold minimizes
 loss subject to at most 5% of validation attempts being alerted; all tied scores are treated together. In stages III
 and IV, zero selected alerts becomes an explicit no-alert policy. The validation constraint does not guarantee
 identical test workload.
 The study reports normalized cost and savings per 1,000 attempts, AP, ROC-AUC, precision, recall, F1, alert
 rate and probability diagnostics. Brier score is the mean squared difference between predicted probability and
 binary label. For stage III, folds are averaged within seed before inference to avoid treating overlapping
 training windows as independent. Stage IV computes paired differences on one final test period per seed. The
 approximate interval is mean ± t(0.975, n−1) × sample standard deviation / √n. Sensitivity intervals are
 exploratory and not multiplicity-adjusted.
 Stage IV uses fixed probability bins for descriptive calibration curves, TreeSHAP raw-log-odds attributions
 for XGBoost, and standardized linear independent-background attributions for Logistic Regression, using 256
 fixed held-out examples per seed. Feature and score stability diagnostics use PSI with validation quantile bins
 and count smoothing. No causal meaning is assigned to feature attributions. PSI cannot detect a change in
 labels when features and scores remain unchanged.

 5. Results
 5.1 Stage I: operational intelligence and baseline models
 The initial dataset generated 100,000 attempts and KES 329,143,635.31 in gross attempted volume. Approval,
 failure and pending rates were 88.16%, 8.41% and 3.43%, respectively. There were 684 settlement exceptions,
 with an absolute discrepancy of KES 42,480. These are simulated operational outcomes, not real provider
 performance or revenue.

 6. Explainability, calibration and stability
 Raw class-weighted scores were poorly calibrated as probabilities. Under the original mechanism, mean Brier
 score declined from 0.228539 to 0.017236 for Logistic Regression and from 0.216671 to 0.017241 for
 XGBoost. Under stress the corresponding changes were 0.230154 to 0.023946 and 0.218431 to 0.023931.
 These reductions demonstrate the importance of calibration for this experiment, but calibrated scores remain
 close to rare-event prevalence and do not establish clinical or financial usefulness.

 Recent transaction count was the largest mean attribution in both models. XGBoost assigned substantial
 importance to retry intensity, while Logistic Regression gave relatively more attribution to attempt count.
 Since these variables are correlated and enter the constructed label mechanism, the results are explanatory of
 model behaviour rather than independent causal evidence. Attribution scales and background assumptions
 differ between model families, so direct magnitude comparisons require caution.
 The workflow generated calibration plots, attribution stability charts and PSI diagnostics. Numeric PSI
 summaries were not exposed in the inspected execution logs, so no claim of detected or absent drift is made
 here. The repository provides the corresponding artifact for inspection.

 7. Discussion
 The four stages show why a single test ranking should not determine model selection. The V2 XGBoost
 advantage over Random Forest survived V3 replication, yet the stronger V4 comparison favoured Logistic
 Regression in mean cost under the original mechanism. This is plausible because the original labels are
 generated from a relatively simple additive logistic score. A flexible learner may capture nonlinearities, but
 additional complexity need not improve economic outcomes when signals are weak and review capacity is
 restricted.
 The stage IV stress test removes the statistically supported difference. Across the research, the strongest
 observed baseline recall is still low at operational thresholds: V4 Logistic Regression detects only about 14%
 of positives under the original mechanism. Thus even the better observed cost result is not a justification for
 using the model as the sole transaction-control mechanism. Human review, business rules and independent
 real-world assessment remain necessary.






 8. Limitations and implications
 The fundamental limitation is external validity. All observations, provider identities, risk labels and costs are
 simulated. The 31 distinct random seeds generate new draws from prescribed mechanisms rather than different
 financial institutions or actual customer populations. The study therefore establishes Monte Carlo reliability
 conditional on its generator and evaluation assumptions, not fraud prevalence, causal determinants, real
 savings or actual PSP performance.
 The cost function assumes constant costs per review and missed positive, without transaction-specific
 exposure, recovery probability, investigation delay, staffing constraints or false-positive customer impacts. A
 validation alert-rate constraint is not a guaranteed production capacity limit. The thresholds and the practical
 similarity margin are analyst choices. The stress mechanism is one alternative label process, not a
 representative model of adversarial behaviour. The seed-level t intervals are approximate; exploratory
 comparisons are unadjusted for multiplicity.
 Further limitations include the lack of customer-level clustering, real event ordering, independent label
 adjudication, stakeholder-approved loss estimates, and a fully pinned execution environment. The manuscript
 transcribes successfully completed GitHub workflow logs and repository protocols; underlying output files
 have not been independently recomputed or audited for this manuscript. The dashboard exists as source code
 but public deployment is not verified.
 Future work should acquire appropriately authorised and de-identified transaction data, document label
 provenance and feature availability, evaluate account-level and temporal leakage, estimate real review and loss
 costs, and validate on a later independent period or institution. A pre-registered external comparison and
 confidence intervals that account for clustering would strengthen claims. Monitoring should assess actual alert
 workload, calibration degradation and outcome-based concept drift, rather than rely solely on PSI.

 9. Conclusion
 Project Sentinel demonstrates a reproducible methodology for integrating payment operations analytics, model
 comparison, cost-sensitive decisions, temporal validation and explainability. The pre-specified V3 comparison
 supports a simulated XGBoost cost advantage over Random Forest; the fresh V4 comparison supports a
 Logistic Regression cost advantage over XGBoost under the original mechanism, but neither dominates under
 the alternative mechanism. The results argue for using simple baselines, calibrated probabilities, independent
 replication and explicit operational cost assumptions before selecting complex machine learning models. No
 model is established as universally superior or suitable for production fraud detection.
